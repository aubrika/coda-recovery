# Do sperm whales string their calls together in a meaningful order? — in plain language

## The animals and their "words"

Sperm whales talk in short bursts of clicks — like Morse code. Each patterned
burst is called a **coda**, and each population uses a small set of distinct coda
patterns: about 21 different ones in the Caribbean group we studied, and several
dozen in the Pacific groups. Think of these coda types as a little **vocabulary**
of "words."

Whales are also sorted into **clans** — large cultural groups that share a
vocabulary, a bit like people who share a language. Two clans can use many of the
same coda "words" but be socially separate.

## The question

Everyone already knows *which* codas each clan uses and *how often*. We asked a
different question:

> Does the **order** matter? When a whale picks its next coda, does the choice
> depend on what came just before — so that a coda has a **role in the sequence**,
> the way "the" in English is usually followed by a noun?

If order carries information, you should be able to **identify a coda just from
its position in the back-and-forth** — from what tends to come before and after
it — *without listening to the coda itself*. That would be a hint of something
grammar-like. If order carries nothing, then a coda is just a coda, and only its
sound and its frequency matter.

## How we tested it

1. Take one clan's recordings and **split them into two independent halves**.
2. **Hide the names** of the coda types in both halves.
3. Ask a computer to **re-match** the anonymized codas between the two halves,
   using *only* each coda's pattern of what-follows-what — its position in the
   sequence.
4. Count how many of the (say) 15 coda types it gets right.

The catch: getting a lot right *sounds* impressive, but it could be a cheat. If
one coda is very common in both halves, you can match it just by noticing it's
common — no sense of "order" required at all. So we need a way to subtract out
that cheat. That's the **real-vs-shuffled** comparison, and it's the heart of the
whole thing (explained below).

## What we found

A **small but genuine** effect — much smaller than it first looked, and not
universal:

- In the **original Caribbean clan**, sequence order really does carry a little
  extra identity: about **1–2 extra codas out of 15** are identifiable from their
  order alone, beyond what you'd get from frequency. This holds up under a strict
  test.
- The same faint signal appears in **2 of the 7 Pacific clans**, is borderline in
  2 more, and is **absent in 3**. So it's real in places, but **not a universal
  law** of sperm whale communication.
- Curiously, it isn't about how much data we had — the clan with the *least* data
  showed the *strongest* effect. It seems to be a real difference between clans.
- **Across two different clans, the order never transfers.** The sequencing that
  helps identify a coda inside its own clan tells you nothing about another clan.

## What it means

Mostly, a sperm whale coda's identity lives in **how it sounds** and **how often
it's used** — not strongly in where it sits in a sequence. But there's a **faint,
real trace of sequential structure** in some clans, including the original one.
It's subtle, it varies between clans, and it does not carry across clans.

So: not "whale grammar" in any bold sense — but a small, honest signal that a few
clans put their codas in slightly non-random orders, in a way that's specific to
each clan. As far as we can tell from the published literature, **no one has asked
this particular question before** (comparing the *ordering* of coda types across
clans), so even the modest answer is new ground.

## Honest limits

- The effect is **small** and only clears the bar in some clans.
- The recordings are **limited** — a few thousand codas per clan — so faint
  signals are hard to measure precisely.
- This says **nothing about meaning**: we're asking whether order is *structured*,
  not what any coda "means."
